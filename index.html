Readers that have to prove every pattern they learn

Paste a contract and it reads it for the dates, the parties, the governing law, the renewal and notice terms, the money caps, and ten kinds of clause. Paste a receipt and it reads the line items, subtotal, tax, service charge, discount, total, cash and change, then checks them against each other by plain arithmetic. Paste a customer's message and it routes it to one of 77 banking teams, and flags it when the customer wants a human or something was lost or stolen. Each answer comes with the exact pattern that found it. When no proven pattern fits, it says unknown instead of guessing. It answers only what it has proven, and the figures just below say how much that is today.

The readers learn every night, on this laptop: the contract reader from 510 real commercial contracts, the receipt reader from 1,000 real receipts, the message router from 16,163 real customer messages. A new pattern is kept only if it beats a cheap method on documents it has never seen, and then passes a second exam on documents nobody has looked at. Every other proposed pattern is thrown away, and the reason is written down. Reading your document never calls any model: it only applies the proven patterns.

How it works

Each night runs the same seven steps, cheapest first. The model only ever proposes; plain code decides.

  1. Gather. The 510 contracts of CUAD, a public set filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission and marked up by trained reviewers (The Atticus Project, CC BY 4.0). Plain code finds every candidate of each kind: every date, every amount, every duration, every jurisdiction, every company name, every sentence.
  2. Propose, cheapest first. First the free tiers: a recipe lookup (words that worked for a sibling question, like another date field) and three miners that count which nearby words go with a right answer: the most precise single words, the most-used words that are still right four times in five, and pairs of words. Then, only for questions the free tiers kept nothing for, the local 7B model (qwen2.5, running on this laptop) reads 20 right and 20 wrong examples and writes rules. Last, and only when both failed, the paid tier may be asked, behind its wall.
  3. Test. A pattern is one or two words near a candidate: "WHEN governed THEN governing law". It must fire on at least five candidates in contracts it never saw and be right 80% of the time, beat the commonest answer by 0.2, stay within its competence, and pass a second exam on contracts nobody looked at. Then a second gate checks whole contracts: on the contracts where it fires, it must be right in 80% of them and beat the cheap method (take the first date, the first jurisdiction, ctrl-F for the clause's word) by 0.10.
  4. Keep or drop. A winner enters the library with its scores and a receipt. A loser is dropped and its reason is written down. A near-copy of a kept pattern is refused.
  5. Cache how it was built. Which proposer found it, from which recipe, at what cost. The next night tries that recipe on sibling questions first, for free.
  6. Measure. Coverage and precision on 60 contracts that are never used for anything else.
  7. Report. One line in the keeper's morning brief: kept, dropped and why, the coverage climb, the credit spent and what it bought, and what needs the keeper. Nothing goes outward at night.

The fresh exam: no test is ever used twice

A library that is tested on the same contracts every night slowly learns the test. So the 510 contracts are put in one fixed order, and cut once:

Night n is tested on batches 2n and 2n+1, which no earlier night has seen; afterwards they only ever train. When the batches run out, the library says so and stops adding patterns until fresh contracts arrive. It never re-uses an exam.

The credit wall (the paid tier)

This is on the builder's side, not yours. Reading a contract never calls any model and costs nothing. The paid tier is only one of the ways new patterns are proposed while the library is grown: a frontier model reached through the library keeper's own Claude subscription (today AI Native Solutions'; yours only if you grow the library on your own contracts and choose to switch it on), through the official command-line tool. A subscription has no price per call, so the wall meters each call in what it would cost on the published API price list ("API-equivalent").

  • Last tier only. A question may go to it only if the free tiers kept nothing for it tonight and the local model tried and nothing it wrote passed.
  • Reserved before it goes out. The most the call could cost is written to the ledger on disk first, and checked against the day's cap and a hard stop. The same ceiling is handed to the tool as its own budget limit.
  • Settled after. Priced from the usage the tool reports, rounded up. If the usage cannot be read, the whole ceiling is charged.
  • A stop that stays stopped. Reaching the stop switches the tier off until the keeper raises it.
  • Off until the keeper switches it on. Spending is the keeper's decision, and so is the daily cap.

Against what you pay now

Security, data protection and audit

  • Your documents stay with you. The reader runs in the browser or on one machine. There is no server and no upload. Under UK GDPR and GDPR that means no third-party processor for the documents.
  • Every answer is auditable. It names the pattern that produced it, and every pattern carries its exam scores and a receipt. An auditor can re-run the whole night from the public data and get the same bytes.
  • It abstains rather than guesses. No pattern fires, no answer. The share it answers is measured and published.
  • Your reading never touches a paid model. The paid tier only proposes patterns while the library is grown, on the builder's side (see the credit wall): last resort, capped, ledgered, off unless switched on.

Deployment and roll-out

  1. Week 1, try it on your own contracts. Paste them into this page. Nothing to install and no IT work: the nightly learning runs on the builder's machine, and the page picks up each new night's patterns. Nothing leaves your browser.
  2. Week 2, grow it on yours. Give it your own contracts with the answers marked, in the same format as the public set, and the same nightly loop learns your house style on your machine. Your data never joins the public set.
  3. Week 3, wire it in. The library is one small file of patterns and the reader is one function, read(text, patterns): put it in front of your review queue. Answered questions arrive with their sentence; unknown ones go to your reviewers or your model, as today.

Ownership, support and exit

The code is MIT licensed and the library is plain files you keep. No lock-in: stop using it and you still own every pattern and every receipt. Built and supported by AI Native Solutions (ainativesolutions@gmail.com); terms are agreed one to one.

Risks, answered straight

  • It answers only part of each contract today. The share is on this page, measured, and it grows by night. Everything it does not answer goes to people or a model exactly as now.
  • It learned on US commercial contracts. Other document types (leases, employment, UK forms) need their own marked-up examples; the method is the same.
  • It is not legal advice. It finds and points; a qualified person decides.
  • Patterns can be wrong. Each one was right at least 80% of the time on contracts it never saw, and the measured precision is published. The sentence comes with the answer, so a wrong one is caught by reading that sentence.
  • The receipt reader learned on Indonesian shop and restaurant receipts. UK VAT receipts and supplier invoices need their own marked-up examples; the method and the check are the same.
  • It reads text, not photos. A receipt photo goes through text recognition first; the library reads the lines that come out.
  • No dates, suppliers or payment terms on receipts. The public set had them removed before it was published, so this library does not read them and does not claim to.
  • The message router learned on one bank's kind of questions. Its 77 teams are Banking77's intents. Your teams and your customers' words need your own labelled messages; the method is the same.
  • No flag does not mean safe. A message with no "wants a human" or "lost or stolen" flag goes to the normal queue, where a person reads it as today.

The case for the board

White paper: the method and the sealed claims

The problem. Models that read contracts are opaque, cost money per page, and are confidently wrong some of the time. A rule-based reader is cheap and readable, but hand-written rules do not grow.

The method. Grow the rules, but let nothing in unproven. Each night, cheap proposers (counting miners, the recipes of past winners) and then a local model propose short rules; deterministic code tests each one on contracts the proposers never saw, against a cheap method, by margins written down before any code existed, and then again on a second set nobody has looked at. The exam batches are used once. Coverage is measured on contracts that never take part in any decision. Every kept rule carries its scores and a receipt; every dropped rule carries its reason.

The bars were sealed first. The claims and every number in them were committed before any code (CLAIMS.md, seal 1). A claim that misses its bar is kept and shown as failed.

ClaimWhat it saysVerdict, from the records

Library 2, receipts. The same night and the same two gates, on its own sealed bars (seal 2, committed before its code): seven amounts read one per receipt and the line items read many per receipt, against ctrl-F, on a fresh exam of 40 receipts per batch. On top sits a check that is not learned at all: three sums over the library's own answers. The check was itself tested first on the experts' answers, and a part that failed that test would have been dropped. A receipt the check marks reconciled is claimed to be right at least 95% of the time; one with a wrong amount is claimed to be flagged at least half the time. Both claims are decided on the measuring receipts once enough of them are answered, and shown above.

Library 3, messages. Routing and triage, the same night and the same two gates, on their own sealed bars (seal 3): each of 77 teams is a question asked of every message, against a keyword router built from the teams' own names; the two triage flags against ctrl-F. The router is measured whole every night: how many messages it routed, how many right, how many with a conflict, and the keyword router's own figures on the same messages.

A flaw found and fixed in the open (seal 4). Every library cut its exams from one fixed order of its documents, made by hashing their ids. For ids that are consecutive row numbers, that hash did not shuffle: library 3's routing exams held only about 50 of the 77 teams, and library 2's receipt meter came from 8 of 20 parts of its file. Libraries 2 and 3 now order by a hash that mixes (SHA-256); the nights made under the old order are kept, re-derived and shown, and counted toward nothing. Library 1 was not affected.

What it does not do. It does not read scanned images; it does not decide anything; it does not learn from your documents unless you run it on your own machine with your consent.

Re-run it. The repository holds the code, the night records and a tool that re-derives every night byte for byte from the public contracts: node tools/rederive.mjs CUADv1.json. GitHub's own computers do this on every change.
